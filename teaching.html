2. Teaching and student learning

My teaching contribution is presented through my philosophy and the five evaluation elements identified by the Committee. The detailed course record is in Appendix A11; teaching evidence is organized in Appendix B.

2.1 Teaching philosophy

2.1.1 What I aim to do

My aim is to help students understand why a method works, when it is appropriate, and what its limitations are. In computing and AI, obtaining output is only part of learning: students also need to explain their choices, interpret results, and recognize when a method does not answer the question they are investigating. My teaching ranges from introductory programming, where programming is a practical way to reason about information for students taking computing as part of a broader education, to advanced AI and data analysis, where mathematical understanding, algorithmic reasoning, and careful evaluation matter more.

2.1.2 Why I take this approach

I connect concepts, implementation, and interpretation because students need all three to use computing responsibly: a working program can conceal weak understanding, and an explanation without practical application can leave students unable to use what they have learned. My assessment principles accordingly emphasize reasoning as well as implementation, with clear expectations and opportunities to learn from feedback, which is reflected in the project-based design of CS446, CS307, CS316, and CS504.

2.1.3 Why I consider the approach effective

I consider the approach effective when students can explain a method in their own words, justify their choices, apply it to a new problem, and identify limitations in their results. My 2026 Excellence in Teaching Award and the student research accomplishments in Section 2.2.4 are relevant indicators, but they do not by themselves establish the effect of anyone teaching practice; that connection is substantiated through the evaluation record in Section 2.2.2 and Appendix B1.

A concrete illustration is the Honor’s project on deep learning for object detection, completed in 2025. The student first met this material in CS446 (Intelligent Systems and Neural Networks), Fall 2023, a course I developed around project-based work with real datasets. Their experience in the course motivated them to undertake an Honor’s project in the area, which I supervised through problem formulation, implementation, evaluation, and writing of a neural-network object-detection system. They had no prior experience training neural networks; by the end she had built and evaluated an object-detection model on her own. This is a single example rather than proof of general effect, but it connects the course-level aim in Section 2.1.1, that students understand and justify their methods rather than only run them, to a sustained piece of independent work. She subsequently completed a computer vision and machine learning internship at an autonomous-systems company (USA) and began an MSc at York University. Supporting records are in Appendix B7.1.

2.2 Highlight teaching effectiveness

2.2.1 Preparation and effective course delivery

My Bishop's teaching record spans data visualization, programming for data science, image processing, databases, general computing, introductory AI, and neural networks. I developed CS219 around Python for students from computer science and other disciplines; developed CS446 in intelligent systems and neural networks; and developed CS320 and CS520 around natural language processing and large language models. These contributions support both access to computing and progression into advanced study. The course inventory in Appendix B1 records, for each offering, the course and term and the student evaluation scores, compared with the institutional average at Bishop’s and with the department and college averages at SQU. The resource portfolio in Appendix B2 connects selected syllabi and assessments with the learning outcomes they address.

My lectures follow a consistent structure, which I use in e.g., CS405/CS505. I begin by asking students to recall briefly what we covered in the previous lecture, which refreshes the material and shows me what has stuck. I then present a new problem situation and give students two to three minutes to think about a possible solution. When a student offers a tentative answer, I invite the class to comment on it, so that students practice evaluating reasoning, not only producing answers. Only then do I state the lecture's objectives and outline. From there I formalize the problem mathematically and introduce the tool best suited to solving it. I also present the main alternatives, explain why I favor the first tool, and discuss the strengths and limitations of each. Finally, I show how the chosen method is implemented algorithmically, followed by a short program that solves the problem, so students see the path from the problem to mathematics to working code. Sometimes when students appear lost, I return to the previous step and re-explain it with a different example before moving on. Some selected slides in Appendix B2 (a selection, not the complete set) illustrate the structure described above of my cross-listed course CS405/CS505 given in Fall 2026 are shown in Appendix B2.

2.2.2 Student feedback and changes to practice

Bishop's course evaluations since Fall 2023 (Appendix B1) place my overall score above the institutional average in six of eight evaluated course offerings (CS504 in Fall 2023; CS503 in Winter 2024 and Winter 2025; CS219 in Winter 2025; CS307 and CS316 in Fall 2025). Two offerings fell slightly below: CS446 in Winter 2025 and, CS462/CS562 in Winter 2024 my first cross-listed BSc/MSc course. In that course students rated the instructor (3.38) lower than the course content (4.03), which suggests the problem was my delivery rather than the material: I had not yet found a pace and level of explanation that served both groups, and I did not keep the class well in hand. I have not taught CS462/CS562 since, so I applied the lesson to CS405/CS505 (Fall 2026) by structuring each lecture so that both levels are served and marking where each group should concentrate, as the slides in Appendix B2 show. Because that course is in its first term, I cannot yet show evaluation evidence of improvement and will report it when available. If I teach CS462/CS562 again, I will use the same approach. Winter 2026 was not evaluated because too few students completed the survey.

2.2.4 Supervision and student accomplishments

Supervision is also part of my teaching contribution. At Bishop's, examples include a completed honor’s project on object detection, a completed USRA project on EEG preprocessing, and master's projects in meta-unsupervised learning, seizure detection, medical report summarization, and anomaly detection. The completed Bishop's master's project on meta-unsupervised learning led to a co-authored paper at Canadian AI 2025 (C10), and two of my students won Best Conference Student Paper Awards in 2025 — one at the 38th Canadian AI Conference and one at IEEE BDDM (Appendix D.2). Both award-winning papers began as project-track Master’s work. In both cases I encouraged the student to move from the project track to the thesis track once their work showed a result worth developing into a full research paper, although publication is not required of MSc students. I supervised them through that additional work, and both went on to publish and to win a best paper award. The papers and award notices are in Appendices D2 and B7.

At Sultan Qaboos University (2017–2023), some students I supervised also reached recognized outcomes. An MSc thesis I supervised received the Best Supervised MSc Thesis award of the College of Science in 2020, and the student published the work with me at the IEEE ICIoT 2020 conference (C15, Appendix A6.2). Two PhD students I supervised won the Best Published Paper award of Sultan Qaboos University, in 2023 and in 2024 (Appendix D2). I also supervised a BSc final-year project and recommended the student for a full scholarship to an MSc at Aalto University, which she received; we later published a paper together at ACM Multimedia 2025, an A*-ranked ACM conference (source: ICORE 2026). Together with the Bishop's examples above, these outcomes are documented in Section 4 and Appendices B7 and D2.

2.3 Apply disciplinary knowledge

2.3.1 Maintaining and interpreting disciplinary knowledge

My research in representation learning, AI, image analysis, and medical data analysis informs the questions I bring into teaching, limited labels, uncertain predictions, representation choices, and interpretation. I aim to translate disciplinary material into explanations and tasks appropriate to students' preparation rather than assume that research expertise alone ensures effective teaching. Recorded examples include adding Bayesian learning to CS316, map and network visualization to CS503, and new practical topics to CS504.

2.3.2 Teaching strategies and new resources

My approach moves from the question a method addresses its assumptions, operation, and evaluation, connecting mathematical ideas with examples students can inspect and implement. CS219 and CS320 provide accessible entry points for students from varied backgrounds, while CS505 and CS520 provide room for deeper analysis for students progressing further.

For instance, in Fall 2026 I taught CS405/CS505, a cross-listed course taken by both BSc and MSc students. The selected slides in Appendix B2 (a selection, not the complete set) illustrate the flow of my lectures and how I serve two levels of students in the same room: the slides mark which material is core for everyone and where each group should concentrate, with BSc students focusing on e.g., concepts and implementation and MSc students on e.g., mathematical formalization and analysis. As the course is in its first term, there are no student evaluations yet; I will report them, and any changes I make in response, in future updates to this dossier.

2.3.3 Professional development and engagement with literature

In September 2021 I attended the workshop “Active learning: the key to student learning”, led by Prof. Grahame Bilbow (Grange Education), at Sultan Qaboos University on 23 September 2021, Appendix B6. The idea I kept was to begin with a short recall of the previous lecture and to give students quick chances to test their understanding during class. I apply it at Bishop’s through the structure of my lectures: students first recall the previous lecture, then work for two or three minutes on a new problem before I present the method (Section 2.2.1; slides in Appendix B2). Unsolicited student comments are consistent with this approach: one student described the classes as “lively and informative” (CS307, Fall 2025), and another credited the weekly assignments with “a great hands-on learning experience” and a stronger ability to choose a suitable algorithm for a problem (CS504, CS503 and CS462/CS562, 2023–24; Appendix B1).

2.5.1 Engagement and support for varied preparation

Teaching at two institutions, and teaching Python to students across disciplines, informs my attention to varied preparation. CS219 was designed specifically to give students without a computer science background an accessible route into programming, and I have provided flexible exam scheduling and assignment deadlines to students with family responsibilities.

2.5.2 Assessment and skill development

My assessment principles are to make expectations clear and evaluate understanding as well as implementation.
