1. Course Description

Language is the primary medium through which humans communicate, reason, and record knowledge. Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the subfield of Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence concerned with enabling computers to understand, process, and generate human language. From search engines and spam filters to machine translation and intelligent assistants, NLP underlies a broad range of technologies that shape modern business and daily life.

CS320 provides a rigorous yet accessible introduction to the core concepts, algorithms, and practical tools of NLP. The course is designed for a mixed audience of Computer Science (BSc CS) and Business/Information Technology (BSc BAIT) students at the third-year undergraduate level. Mathematical content is presented at a moderate level: formal notation is used where appropriate, but every formula is accompanied by a plain-language explanation, and no result is expected to be derived from first principles. The emphasis is on conceptual understanding and practical application.

The course follows the classical NLP pipeline — from raw text to intelligent applications — progressing from statistical foundations through neural sequence models. It stops deliberately before the Transformer architecture, which is the entry point of CS 520 level (Large Language Models). Students who complete CS320 will have the background necessary to understand and critically evaluate modern language technology, including the limitations and ethical concerns it raises.

Scope

This course covers: text preprocessing, statistical language models, vector space representations, word embeddings, text classification, sequence labeling, syntactic parsing, recurrent neural networks, the encoder-decoder framework with attention, and NLP ethics.

This course does not cover (reserved for CS 520): Transformer architectures, BERT, GPT and other large language models, fine-tuning, prompt engineering, and retrieval-augmented generation.

2. Learning Outcomes

Upon successful completion of CS320, students will be able to:

Knowledge and Understanding

  • Explain the main challenges of NLP and the role of the different levels of linguistic analysis.
  • Describe statistical n-gram language models and the need for smoothing.
  • Distinguish sparse and dense text representations and justify their use.
  • Explain the evolution from RNNs/LSTMs to attention-based models.

Skills and Application

Build a complete text preprocessing pipeline using Python, NLTK and/or spaCy.

Implement and evaluate classical text classifiers on real datasets.

Apply pre-trained word embeddings for similarity and analogy tasks.

Design, implement and present a small NLP project.

Professional and Ethical Awareness

Identify sources of bias in NLP systems and discuss ethical and regulatory considerations (including relevant Canadian frameworks).

4.3 Policies

Late submissions: A penalty of 10 % per calendar day will be applied to late assignments, up to a maximum of three days. Assignments submitted more than three days late will receive a grade of zero. Extensions may be granted if requested in writing before the original deadline.

Missed midterm: A student who misses the midterm examination due to a documented medical or family emergency should contact the instructor within 48 hours. A make-up examination or grade reallocation will be arranged at the instructor's discretion.

5. Required and Recommended References

  • Jurafsky, D., & Martin, J. H. (2024). Speech and Language Processing (3rd ed. draft). Available free of charge at: https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/
  • Howard, J., & Gugger, S. (2020). Practical Deep Learning for Coders. fast.ai. Available free of charge at: https://course.fast.ai

Students are not required to purchase any textbook. All required readings are freely available online or will be posted on Moodle.

This resource is particularly recommended for BAIT students and for students who find the mathematical presentation of Jurafsky and Martin challenging. The fast.ai course is designed from the outset for practitioners without a strong mathematical background: it is code-first, application-first, and explicitly avoids unnecessary formalism.

The two references are complementary: Jurafsky and Martin provides the theoretical rigour required for a university course, while fast.ai provides an alternative entry point for students who prefer to see working code before formal definitions. Students are encouraged to consult whichever resource aids their understanding; both are acceptable as secondary citations in assignments and the final project report

6. Course Policies

6.1 Academic Integrity

All work submitted for evaluation must be the student's own. Collaboration is encouraged for conceptual understanding, but all code, written analyses, and reports must be produced independently, unless explicit pair work is authorized.

Use of generative AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot) must be disclosed in any submission. Undisclosed use of AI-generated content constitutes academic dishonesty under the Bishop's University Academic Integrity Policy. Students are responsible for familiarizing themselves with that policy.

Plagiarism detection software may be used to verify originality. Suspected violations will be reported to the Academic Integrity Committee.

6.2 Accessibility and Accommodation

Bishop's University is committed to providing equitable access to education for all students. Students with documented disabilities or other circumstances requiring accommodation should contact the SAS Office and provide official documentation. Students are encouraged to notify the instructor of their accommodation plan within the first two weeks of the term so that appropriate arrangements can be made in a timely manner.

6.3 Attendance

Regular attendance at lectures sessions is expected. Lecture slides and any supplementary materials will be posted on Moodle; however, in-class discussion, worked examples, and live coding demonstrations are integral parts of the learning experience and will not be recorded. Students who miss a class are responsible for obtaining notes from a classmate and reviewing posted materials.

6.4 Communication and Office Hours

The instructor will respond to email enquiries within two business days. For questions of general interest, students are strongly encouraged to use the course discussion forum on Moodle; answers provided there benefit all students. Office hours will be posted on Moodle at the beginning of the term. Students requiring assistance outside of office hours may arrange an appointment by email.

6.5 Inclusive Learning Environment

This course is designed to be accessible to both Computer Science and BAIT students. Each group brings different strengths: CS students may have stronger programming and mathematical foundations, while BAIT students may have stronger domain knowledge in business applications of technology. These differences are a resource, not a barrier. Questions are always welcome, and no level of question is considered too basic. Students are expected to treat their peers with respect and professionalism.
